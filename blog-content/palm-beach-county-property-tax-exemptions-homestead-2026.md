# Palm Beach County Homestead Exemption Guide 2026

Palm Beach County homeowners who file for every exemption they qualify for can legally cut thousands of dollars from their annual tax bill — but only if they apply before the March 1 deadline and understand how the Save Our Homes cap, portability, and senior exemptions actually stack together.

Florida offers several layers of property tax relief: the standard homestead exemption, the Save Our Homes (SOH) assessment cap, additional exemptions for seniors and people with disabilities, and a portability benefit that lets you carry your accumulated tax savings from one home to another. Each has its own eligibility rules, filing deadlines, and dollar impact. This guide walks through all of them using 2026 figures from the Palm Beach County Property Appraiser's Office (pbcpao.gov), the Florida Legislature (flsenate.gov, 2026), and the Florida Department of Revenue.

---

## What Is the Florida Homestead Exemption?

The homestead exemption is the foundation of Florida's property tax relief system. Under Florida Statutes §196.031, as published by the Florida Legislature (flsenate.gov, 2026), a primary-residence homeowner qualifies for up to $50,000 in reduced taxable value, applied in two layers:

- **First $25,000** applies to all tax levies (county, school board, city, special districts).
- **Second $25,000** applies only to non-school levies, on assessed value between $50,000 and $75,000.

In practice, a home assessed at $350,000 with full homestead has its taxable value reduced to $300,000 for school-related levies and $325,000 for county and municipal levies. The split matters because the school millage in Palm Beach County runs about 5 to 6 mills — roughly a third of the total tax load, according to the Palm Beach County Tax Collector (pbctax.com, 2026).

According to the Palm Beach County Property Appraiser (pbcpao.gov, 2026), the average homesteaded property saves approximately $773 per year versus an identical non-homesteaded property at current countywide millage rates. On a higher-value home the savings scale with the millage math: at a blended effective rate of about 18 mills on non-school levies, a $50,000 reduction in taxable value is worth roughly $900 a year in avoided county and city taxes.

**Who qualifies:** You must own the property and make it your permanent primary residence as of January 1 of the tax year. You cannot hold an active homestead exemption in another state.

## The Save Our Homes Assessment Cap

Homestead status does more than reduce taxable value by $50,000 — it also triggers the Save Our Homes (SOH) assessment cap, the protection that makes long-term ownership in Florida dramatically cheaper over time.

Under Florida law (Florida Statutes §193.155), once a property receives a homestead exemption, the assessed value cannot increase by more than 3% per year or the annual change in the Consumer Price Index, whichever is lower. According to the Florida Department of Revenue (January 2026), the SOH cap for 2026 is set at **2.7%**, reflecting the CPI coming in below the 3% ceiling.

The real-world effect compounds quickly. A home purchased in 2018 for $400,000 that appreciated to $750,000 in market value by 2026 would, under SOH, have an assessed value capped at roughly $480,000–$500,000 depending on the year-by-year CPI adjustments — not $750,000. The difference between assessed value and market value is called the SOH differential, and it is the asset that portability lets you transfer.

Non-homesteaded properties — investment properties, second homes, vacant land — face a different cap: 10% per year for non-school levies, per Florida Statutes §193.1554 (Florida Legislature, flsenate.gov, 2026). They do not benefit from SOH.

## Portability: Taking Your Tax Savings With You

Homeowners who sell a homesteaded property and buy a new one in Florida can transfer their accumulated SOH differential to the new homestead. This is called portability, governed by Florida Statutes §193.155(8).

**How it works:**

1. You owned a home with a market value of $600,000 and an SOH-capped assessed value of $380,000. Your SOH differential is $220,000.
2. You sell that home and buy a new one in Palm Beach County worth $700,000.
3. You apply for portability at the new property. The differential transfers, reducing the new home's assessed value by up to $220,000 — so you start at $480,000 rather than $700,000.

According to the Palm Beach County Property Appraiser (pbcpao.gov, 2026), the portability benefit is capped at $500,000. You have **three years** from January 1 of the year you abandon your former homestead to apply portability at the new property. Miss that window and the differential disappears.

Portability is particularly valuable for buyers moving within Palm Beach County or from Broward, Miami-Dade, or another Florida county. It does not transfer across state lines — only Florida-to-Florida moves qualify.

If you are buying a home from a long-term Florida resident, ask your title agent to pull the prior year's TRIM (Truth in Millage) notice for the property. It shows the seller's assessed value versus market value and signals how much tax relief the next owner starts without — since the SOH differential resets to zero for the incoming buyer unless they port their own. According to Florida Realtors (2026), misunderstanding the SOH reset is one of the most common sources of sticker shock in the first full year of Palm Beach County homeownership.

## Additional Exemptions That Stack on Top of Homestead

Florida law layers additional exemptions onto the base homestead benefit for eligible taxpayers. These apply to the taxable value after the homestead reduction, lowering the bill further.

### Senior Exemption (Low-Income)

Homeowners who are 65 or older, have a household adjusted gross income below **$36,614** (per the Florida Department of Revenue 2026 income limit, adjusted annually for CPI), and have held a homestead exemption for at least 25 years on a property worth $250,000 or less (just value) may qualify for a full exemption from county and municipal taxes.

A broader senior exemption — up to an additional $50,000 off assessed value — is available to homeowners 65 or older with income under $36,614, regardless of years of homestead, at the discretion of individual taxing authorities. According to the Palm Beach County Property Appraiser (pbcpao.gov, 2026), Palm Beach County has adopted this additional senior exemption. It applies to county levies only, not the school board portion.

**Filing deadline for senior exemption:** March 1 of the tax year. Applications are available at pbcpao.gov or at the Property Appraiser's offices in West Palm Beach, Belle Glade, and Delray Beach.

### Disability Exemptions

Florida Statutes §196.202 provides a $500 reduction in assessed value for widows, widowers, and people who are legally blind or totally and permanently disabled. That $500 is modest, but the total-disability and combat-related-disability categories go much further.

Under Florida Statutes §196.101, a homeowner who is totally and permanently disabled may qualify for full exemption from all ad valorem taxes — not just a reduction but a zero tax bill. According to the Palm Beach County Property Appraiser (pbcpao.gov, 2026), veterans with a service-connected total disability or who were honorably discharged and are confined to a wheelchair qualify for full exemption under Florida Statutes §196.081. First responders (law enforcement, corrections officers, firefighters, EMTs) who are totally and permanently disabled as a result of service also qualify for full exemption under Florida Statutes §196.102.

Veterans with a partial service-connected disability receive a proportional reduction. A 25% disability rating yields a 25% reduction in assessed value on the homesteaded property, per Florida Statutes §196.24 (Florida Legislature, flsenate.gov, 2026).

### Surviving Spouse of First Responder or Military

Under Florida Statutes §196.081(4), the surviving spouse of a first responder or military member killed in the line of duty may be entirely exempt from property taxes on their homestead. This exemption continues as long as the surviving spouse holds the homestead and does not remarry.

## Filing Deadlines and How to Apply

**Primary homestead exemption:** File by **March 1** of the tax year you want the exemption. You only need to file once — it renews automatically as long as your eligibility doesn't change. If you miss the March 1 deadline for the current year, the Palm Beach County Property Appraiser allows a late filing through September 18 under Florida Statutes §196.011.

**Portability:** File Form DR-501T at the same time as the homestead application on the new property, or separately if applying after the initial filing. The three-year window from January 1 of the year you left your prior homestead is firm.

**Senior and disability exemptions:** These must be applied for separately each year if they involve income verification. Forms are at pbcpao.gov or any Property Appraiser office location.

**New construction buyers:** The homestead exemption is not automatic. According to the Palm Beach County Property Appraiser (pbcpao.gov, 2026), many new-construction buyers assume the builder's tax figures carry forward. They do not. If you closed in 2025 on a new build, you must have filed by March 1, 2026 to receive the exemption for this tax year. Miss it and your first full tax bill reflects the full assessed value.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Homeowners Money

**1. Not applying portability when moving.** Many buyers assume homestead is the only exemption to file. Portability can be worth far more — transferring $150,000 to $300,000 in accumulated SOH differential saves $2,700 to $5,400 per year at current millage rates.

**2. Renting out the home.** Renting your homesteaded property — even for one year — can cause the Property Appraiser to flag it for review. A home that is not owner-occupied as a primary residence does not qualify. If the exemption is found improper, back taxes plus a 50% penalty and interest apply under Florida Statutes §196.011(9).

**3. Forgetting to cancel the exemption when moving.** If you sell or stop using the property as a primary residence, notify the Property Appraiser. Failing to cancel can result in back-tax liability on the receiving end.

**4. Missing the portability window.** The three-year clock starts January 1 of the year you abandon your prior homestead — not the date of sale. Buyers who wait two years to purchase after selling often miss this detail.

**5. Not checking whether the senior income limit applies.** The $36,614 household income ceiling for 2026 is adjusted annually by the Florida Department of Revenue. If your income dropped due to retirement, check every year — you may now qualify for the additional $50,000 senior reduction even if you did not qualify before.

## How Property Taxes Are Calculated in Palm Beach County

Florida uses a millage rate system. One mill equals $1 in tax per $1,000 of taxable value. According to the Palm Beach County Tax Collector (pbctax.com, 2026), the county's total effective millage rate varies by municipality as the county levy, school board levy, and any applicable city or special district levies stack on top of each other.

As an example: a home with a taxable value of $300,000 (after the $50,000 homestead reduction from a $350,000 assessed value) at a blended total millage of 19 mills would owe approximately $5,700 in property taxes. Without the homestead exemption, the same home would owe roughly $6,650 — a difference of about $950 per year, slightly more than the countywide average because the school levy doesn't get the second $25,000.

Over a 10-year ownership period, assuming the SOH cap limits assessment growth to 2.5% per year while market values climb faster, the cumulative tax savings from homestead-plus-SOH on a moderately priced Palm Beach County home routinely exceed $15,000 to $25,000 compared with an equivalent non-homesteaded property, according to analysis based on Palm Beach County Property Appraiser (pbcpao.gov, 2026) assessment data.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I have a homestead exemption in Palm Beach County and another state?**
No. Florida requires the property to be your permanent primary residence. Holding a homestead exemption or its equivalent in another state disqualifies you. Dual-state exemptions are illegal under Florida Statutes §196.011(9) and trigger back taxes plus a 50% penalty.

**Does the homestead exemption transfer to a buyer when I sell?**
No. The exemption terminates on January 1 of the year after the property changes hands. The new owner must file their own application. They may, however, bring their own portability benefit from a prior Florida homestead.

**I inherited a homesteaded property. Do I keep the exemption?**
Not automatically. If you inherit and occupy the home as your primary residence, you can apply for your own homestead exemption and potentially portability. The inherited property's prior SOH differential does not carry forward to you; your cap resets from the current assessed value at the time of transfer.

**What happens to my property tax if I do a major renovation?**
Permitted additions that increase the footprint or add significant value are re-assessed for the new portion. The existing homesteaded value retains its SOH cap; only the new improvement is assessed at market value. Over time the blended rate normalizes.

**Is the March 1 deadline hard, or can I appeal if I miss it?**
March 1 is the standard deadline under Florida Statutes §196.011. The Palm Beach County Property Appraiser accepts late applications through September 18 of the same year. After that, you must wait for the following tax year.

**Does a condo unit qualify for homestead?**
Yes. A condo you own and occupy as your primary Florida residence qualifies for the same homestead exemption, Save Our Homes cap, and portability as a single-family home, per Florida Statutes §196.031. The process and deadline are identical.
